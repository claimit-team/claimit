# ClaimIt Demo Video — Script

A 3-minute film for the Google Cloud Rapid Agent Hackathon (June 2026), submitted to Devpost and uploaded to YouTube and Vimeo. The same cut will live on LinkedIn, X, and YouTube long after the submission window closes.

This is the script of record. Every line below is what the voice will say. Every direction below is how it will be said.

---

## Document conventions

**Version.** 1.0. Locked for first render. Revisions are added to the changelog at the bottom of this document, not woven into the body.

**Runtime.** Target 2:48. Hard cap 3:00 (Devpost only judges the first three minutes). The remaining twelve seconds are for breath, not for content.

**Voice.** One voice carries the entire film. AI-generated. First choice: ElevenLabs (`Rachel` and `Bella` are the candidates we audition first). Fallback: Kokoro via HyperFrames if licensing becomes an issue.

The voice is conversational. Not corporate. Not theatrical. The performance reference is a documentary narrator who has thought about this problem for a long time and decided to just say it plainly. Closer to a friend explaining something than a host announcing something.

**Music.** Specified per section. Silence is a legitimate choice and used deliberately. The whole film never gets loud.

**Subtitles.** Burned in. Identical to the voiceover unless explicitly noted. One line on screen at a time, replacing the previous. Never stacked.

**Timecodes.** Targets, not commitments. Final timing is set during render once the voice is recorded and the cuts are locked.

**Numbers.** Spoken in words ("twenty-six", "fifty dollars", "twelve percent"). Written as numerals on screen ("26", "$50", "12%"). The eye and the ear read different languages.

## ① Opening — 0:00 to 0:06 (6s)

No voice. The film opens with sound and image. Words come later.

**Music.** A single sustained tone, low in the mix. Beneath it, a slow pulse — not a beat, a heartbeat. Think the first six seconds of an Apple keynote: the room is dark, the screen is dark, and something is about to begin.

**Sound design.** Almost nothing. A faint rustle of motion that the storyboard specifies in detail.

**Mood target.** Vast becoming intimate. We begin in the world. We end on one purchase. The cut into the Hook should feel like a lens settling on its subject, not like a scene change.

**Subtitle.** A single musical note glyph (♪) lower-third. No words yet.

**Why no voice here.** The voice arrives at 0:06 and carries fifteen consecutive seconds of attention. If we open with voice, we lose the gear-change at 0:06 that turns the film from atmosphere into address. The silence is a setup. Trust it.

---

## ② Hook — 0:06 to 0:22 (16s)

The first words of the film. The voice is the only thing happening on screen for sixteen seconds. The screen carries the words; the words carry the meaning. Music drops out completely. We are talking to one person.

### Voiceover

**0:06**

> You bought AirPods from Best Buy last week.

*Even. Conversational. Like someone reminding you of a normal Tuesday. No theater on "AirPods" or "Best Buy" — these are facts about the audience, not props. The line should land as recognition, not as a hook.*

**0:10**

> Yesterday, the price dropped.

*Half a beat of silence before "dropped." That micro-pause is the line. Don't punch the verb. Let the silence do the work.*

**0:14**

> You have 8 days to get $50 back.

*State it. Don't sell it. The numbers are not the climax — they are the fact pattern. The voice should sound the same as it did on "AirPods." We are not raising tension yet.*

**0:19**

> Most people never find out.

*Quieter than the previous line. A fraction slower. Land "out" softly and let it sit. Three full seconds of silence follow before the next section begins. The film breathes here.*

### Direction

Sixteen seconds of voice over a near-empty frame is a lot to ask. It works if the voice trusts the audience. It fails the moment the voice tries to perform.

Every line is short on purpose. They are designed to be read aloud at a natural pace — not slowed down for "drama," not sped up for "energy." Read them the way you would read a text message to someone you know.

The arc across the four lines is small. We start at recognition ("you bought") and end at the quiet observation that this is a story that usually ends here ("most people never find out"). The voice should track that drift — almost imperceptibly softer, almost imperceptibly slower — but never collapse into sadness. The voice is not pitying the audience. It is observing.

### Subtitles

Identical to the voiceover, one line at a time. The previous line clears before the next appears. No stacking.

The fourth subtitle ("Most people never find out.") holds on screen through the three-second silence that follows, then fades out as the next section begins.

### Notes for the storyboard

The visuals during this section are deliberately spare. Whatever appears on screen exists to support the words, not to compete with them. Anything visually demanding — motion, color, density — belongs in later sections.

## ③ Why — 0:23 to 0:40 (17s)

The Hook ended on an observation. *Most people never find out.* This section answers the question that observation creates: **why doesn't anyone find out?**

Same voice. Same room. Slightly faster pace — we are explaining now, not setting up. Music returns very gently underneath: a single sustained note that holds through the whole section, then drops out before Section ④.

### Voiceover

**0:23**

> It's not that the policies don't exist.

*Quiet certainty. This is the voice of someone who has looked into this. There is no challenge in the line, only a clarification. Don't emphasize "exist." Emphasize "don't."*

**0:27**

> The price drops quietly. No one tells you.

*The two sentences are one thought. No pause between them. "Quietly" is soft, almost confidential. "No one tells you" is a touch louder, more direct. The line moves from the world's behavior to the audience's experience.*

**0:32**

> Every store plays by different rules. Different windows. Different forms. Different ways to ask.

*Pick up tempo across the list. Each item is one beat, each one a fraction faster than the last. By the fourth "different" the voice has gathered momentum. Don't shout. Don't enumerate. Let the rhythm be the argument.*

**0:38**

> And even when you know, it's rarely worth the time.

*Slow down. This is the line where the audience nods. The voice should sound resigned, not bitter — the way you'd describe a small everyday injustice you've stopped fighting. End on "time" softly. One beat of silence before the section closes.*

### Direction

The section is one continuous argument with three observations underneath it. The argument: *the reason this happens is not laziness. The reason is the system.* The three observations: prices drop silently, rules are fragmented, the effort outweighs the payoff.

The temptation here is to enumerate — "first," "second," "third." Resist it. Enumeration belongs in product specs, not in opening films. The three observations should land as three quiet truths being named in succession, not as a checklist being read aloud.

The single most important sentence in this section is the first one: *It's not that the policies don't exist.* It reframes the entire problem before we describe it. Everything after that sentence is filling in what the audience now suspects. Get this line right and the rest follows.

### Subtitles

Identical to the voiceover with one structural choice. The line "Different windows. Different forms. Different ways to ask." appears as three consecutive subtitles breaking on the periods — each one replacing the previous in sync with the spoken beat. The eye and the ear arrive together. The rhythm becomes visible as well as audible.

Hold the final subtitle ("And even when you know, it's rarely worth the time.") through the one-second silence at the end of the section before fading out.

### Notes for the storyboard

The visuals shift here from atmospheric to typographic. This is the first section in the film where words are the central visual element on screen, not a complement to imagery. Section ③ is, in effect, a typographic chapter — it should look like reading, not like watching.

## ④ Scale — 0:40 to 0:52 (12s)

The first numbers in the film. The Why section explained what's broken. This section measures it.

Two data points. Two beats. Each one on screen long enough to be read.

The voice changes register slightly. Calmer. More clinical. The way a journalist reads a statistic, not the way a marketer announces it. We are not asking the audience to be outraged. We are giving them a number and trusting them to know what it means.

### Voiceover

**0:40**

> Only twelve percent of people ever claim a refund they're owed.

*Quiet. Read it the way you'd read a footnote in a serious report. No outrage. No alarm. The audience does the math themselves.*

**0:45**

> Every year, more than ten billion dollars goes unclaimed.

*A fraction slower on "ten billion." Don't punch the number. Let it land in the audience's head, not in their ears. There is a difference between announcing a number and stating one.*

### Direction

The two lines are the spine of the section. Everything in between is breathing room. After "twelve percent" there should be a two-second hold before "ten billion" begins. After "unclaimed" there should be a one-second hold before the section ends.

The temptation here is to make the numbers feel big. Don't. Big numbers feel bigger when they're stated small. A voice that whispers "ten billion" lands harder than a voice that proclaims it.

The source citations appear on screen, not in the voiceover. We do not read sources aloud. That is an amateur move.

### Subtitles

Each number gets its own subtitle, identical to the voiceover. The source citation appears as smaller text below the main subtitle, in a lower-contrast color:

```
Only 12% of consumers ever claim a refund.
Source: DontPayFull, 2026
```

```
Over $10B in legitimate refunds go unclaimed every year.
Estimated based on U.S. Census Bureau e-commerce data
```

The source citations are visible but do not compete with the headline number. They exist to prove the claim, not to perform.

### Notes for the storyboard

Numbers are the entire visual content of this section. They should appear large, centered, and uninterrupted by ornament. The shader transition between the two numbers is the one moment of visual motion. Everything else is held.

## ⑤ Cut — 0:52 to 0:56 (4s)

The shortest section of the film. The pivot. The Why and Scale sections described a problem. Everything that follows is the answer.

The film changes here. The voice introduces the name of the product for the first and only time before the closing line. The visual changes from typographic to interface. Music returns at slightly higher volume than before — still restrained, but present.

### Voiceover

**0:52**

> This is ClaimIt.

*Confident. One beat. The voice introduces the name and stops. Do not push. The product name is a fact, not an announcement. After "ClaimIt" let two seconds of visual carry the cut into the demo.*

### Direction

This is the only place in the entire film where we name the product before the close. Resist the urge to say it more often. A name said once at the right moment is worth a name said ten times at the wrong ones.

The two-second silence after "ClaimIt" is doing the work of a verbal explanation. The visual is showing the inbox, the receipt, the dashboard. The audience understands without being told. That understanding is the point.

### Subtitles

```
This is ClaimIt.
```

One subtitle, then nothing for two seconds. The next subtitle does not appear until Section ⑥ begins.

### Notes for the storyboard

The transition from the Scale section's last frame to the Gmail interface is the single most important cut in the film. It must feel like the lens settling on the answer. Hard cut, no cross-dissolve. The cut speaks.

## ⑥ Demo — 0:56 to 2:21 (85s)

The heart of the film. One purchase, watched end to end. Best Buy, AirPods, fifty dollars.

The demo is structured as seven beats. Each beat shows one moment of the system working. The voice carries the audience through, but never describes what they are already seeing. The voice's job here is to say what the visual cannot — the policies, the reasoning, the *why* of each step.

Music underneath: a single warm pad, low in the mix, holding through the entire demo. Sound design carries the punctuation — keystroke on send, soft chime on detection, faint whoosh on transition.

### 6.1 — Inbox to dashboard (0:56 to 1:06, 10s)

Visual: a Gmail inbox slides in and settles. A Best Buy order confirmation arrives at the top of the list. The view splits — the inbox stays on the left, a ClaimIt dashboard slides in from the right, and a new card appears: *AirPods Pro · $199 · Monitoring*. A second beat shows an Upload dialog with the line *Also supports PDF & photo upload*.

**0:56**

> Connect your Gmail. ClaimIt watches your inbox.

*State this like you're giving directions. Two short sentences. No "you can" softeners.*

**1:00**

> Order confirmations are detected the moment they arrive.

*Pick up the pace slightly. This is the second beat of the same idea.*

**1:03**

> Or upload a receipt yourself. PDF. Photo.

*The two-word fragments are the rhythm. "PDF. Photo." should feel like checking off the obvious. Don't sell the upload — it's the alternative, not the headline.*

### 6.2 — Extraction (1:06 to 1:14, 8s)

Visual: the Confirm page. On the left, a receipt preview. On the right, extracted fields appearing one by one, each with a green checkmark — Product, SKU, Price Paid, Purchase Date, Membership Tier. At the bottom, a single button: *Confirm and Monitor*.

**1:06**

> Gemini reads every field. Brand. Item. Price paid. Membership tier.

*Read the list cleanly. Each field is one beat. Don't pause between them — let them flow like items being scanned.*

**1:11**

> If anything's uncertain, it asks before monitoring begins.

*Slower. This is the trust line. The audience needs to know that the system has a safety net, but you're not pleading for trust — you're stating a fact about how the product works.*

### 6.3 — The drop (1:14 to 1:24, 10s)

Visual: the dashboard's purchase detail page. The price-history chart is flat. Time fast-forwards — a small "3 days later" label flies across the frame. The chart drops sharply. A banner appears in the top-right: *Price drop detected — $50 refund eligible*. Beneath it, a clean reasoning panel: window remaining, eligibility verdict, refund amount.

**1:14**

> Three days later. The price drops.

*Beat between the two sentences. "Three days later" is a stage direction said out loud. "The price drops" is the event.*

**1:18**

> ClaimIt checks Best Buy's policy. Confirms you're eligible.

*Pick up the tempo. Two actions, two sentences, no air between them. The system is fast — let the voice be fast.*

**1:22**

> Fifty dollars back. Eleven days left.

*Land it. This is the first reward moment in the film. Two short fragments, each its own beat.*

### 6.4 — Four types (1:24 to 1:36, 12s)

Visual: black. Four cards animate in as a 2×2 grid, each one a different claim type with a one-line explanation:

```
Email                    Live Chat Script
For hotels and most      For Best Buy, Macy's,
online retailers         and others

In-Store Guide           Self-Service Walkthrough
For Staples,             For airline rebookings
Costco warehouse
```

After three seconds, three of the cards fade out. The Email card moves to center and scales up.

**1:24**

> Every store accepts claims a different way.

*Confident. This is a thesis statement.*

**1:28**

> An email. A live chat script. An in-store guide. A self-service walkthrough.

*Read the four types as a list. Each one one beat, each beat a fraction faster than the previous. Don't shout.*

**1:33**

> For Best Buy, it's an email. From your address — not ours.

*Land the second sentence. "Not ours" is the only line in this section that is allowed to feel pointed. It is the line that says we are different.*

### 6.5 — The three-pane review (1:36 to 1:46, 10s)

Visual: shader-driven transition into the three-pane Approval UI at `/claims/[id]`. Left pane: the draft email, typing in. Center pane: policy clause, price screenshot, original receipt. Right pane: an empty Assistant chat with a single line of placeholder text — *Ask anything about this claim.*

**1:36**

> Here's the draft.

*State it. The visual is doing the work — your voice is naming the parts.*

**1:39**

> Here's the evidence. The policy clause. The price screenshot. Your receipt.

*Read the list cleanly. Three items, three beats. Don't dwell — the audience can see them.*

**1:43**

> And here's an Assistant that knows everything about this specific claim.

*Land on "this specific claim." That is the differentiator. The Assistant is not generic. It is scoped.*

### 6.6 — Asking the Assistant (1:46 to 2:02, 16s)

Visual: the camera zooms toward the right pane. The Assistant chat fills the frame. A typing animation in the user input field types out a real question. The Assistant streams a real response. As the response completes, the left pane (off-screen now but implied) refreshes with a soft highlight pulse, indicating the draft has been updated with the new context.

**1:46**

> Ask the Assistant anything.

*Direct. Brief. This is the invitation. The action happens in the visual.*

**1:48–1:58** *(silent — the chat action carries it)*

```
User input (typing animation, ~3s):
> Does my Best Buy Plus membership extend the window?

Assistant streaming response (~8s):
> Yes. Plus members get 60 days instead of 15.
> You purchased on May 14. Your window closes
> July 13 — 44 days from now.
```

*No voiceover during the chat exchange. Sound design carries it: a soft typing sound on input, a single chime when the response begins to stream. The chat is the performance.*

**1:58**

> It pulls the policy, your purchase, and the agent's own reasoning. All in one place.

*Quiet. Confident. The line closes the section by naming what just happened. Don't punch "all in one place" — let it close gently.*

### 6.7 — Approve and send (2:02 to 2:21, 19s)

Visual: the camera pulls back to the full three-pane UI. The cursor moves to the *Approve & Submit* button. Click. A confirmation dialog appears — *Submitting claim...* — then resolves. The claim status changes from *Draft pending* to *Submitted*. A toast notification appears in the top-right: *Email sent from your Gmail.* The view transitions to a floating card on a dimmed dashboard: *AirPods Pro · $50 refund · Pending Best Buy response.*

**2:02**

> Approve and send.

*Two words. Then a half-second of silence as the button is clicked on screen.*

**2:05**

> The email goes from your Gmail.

*State it. The next line is where the meaning lands.*

**2:08**

> Your address. Your name. Your account.

*Three fragments, three beats. Pick up tempo slightly. This is the second time we make the point about identity, and it should feel like a closing argument.*

**2:13**

> Best Buy replies to you. Not to us.

*Slow down. The voice is closing a thought. "Not to us" is quiet, almost confidential.*

**2:17**

> Fifty dollars back. Done.

*Land it. "Done" is the period at the end of the demo. After the word, four seconds of silence and visual completion before Section ⑦ begins.*

### Subtitles for Section ⑥

Subtitles match the voiceover line by line. Two structural choices to keep in mind:

The list in 6.4 ("An email. A live chat script. An in-store guide. A self-service walkthrough.") appears as four consecutive subtitles, each replacing the previous in sync with the spoken beat.

The user's typed question in 6.6 ("Does my Best Buy Plus membership extend the window?") appears as a visible chat bubble on screen and does **not** get a separate subtitle. The Assistant's response is part of the visual chat and also does **not** get a subtitle. Only the voiceover lines at 1:46 and 1:58 receive subtitles.

### Direction for the demo as a whole

The demo is the longest section of the film. It has the most happening visually, but the voice should never describe what the visual is already saying. The voice's job here is to say what the visual *cannot* — the policy logic, the differentiator, the *why* of each step.

The tempo across the demo is a slow build. Sections 6.1 through 6.3 are setup. Section 6.4 is the conceptual reveal (four types, one chosen). Sections 6.5 through 6.7 are the climax (review, refine, send). The voice should feel one step calmer than the visual at every point — the system is fast and confident; the voice is the steady hand behind it.

The single most important line in this section is at 2:08: *Your address. Your name. Your account.* That is the one moment in the film where we make a competitive claim. Everything before and after it should support it without restating it.

## ⑦ Reach — 2:21 to 2:35 (14s)

The demo is over. The frame opens up.

Visual: the camera pulls all the way back. We see the full dashboard, a wall of twenty-six platform logos arranged in a clean grid — retail, airlines, hotels. Beside it, a vertical column of *monitored purchases* scrolls slowly, each row showing a different claim type icon (email, chat, in-store, self-service). At the end of the section, every logo on the wall pulses once together.

Music swells slightly. Not triumphant — confirmatory.

### Voiceover

**2:21**

> Best Buy is one platform we ran end to end for this hackathon.

*Honest. State the scope clearly without apologizing for it. "End to end" is the operative phrase.*

**2:26**

> ClaimIt's architecture extends to twenty-six. Retail. Airlines. Hotels.

*Pick up tempo. Read the three categories cleanly. Each one is one beat.*

**2:32**

> All from day one.

*Land it softly. The line says: this is not a roadmap, this is a system. Three words, no flourish.*

### Direction

This section does one job: scale the audience's mental picture of the product without overpromising. The honesty about scope is intentional. We say "one platform we ran end to end" out loud because hackathon evaluators respect transparency, and because the system genuinely does extend to twenty-six — that part is not aspirational, it's how it's built.

The voice here should sound the same as it did in Section ⑥. We are not changing register for the close. The film does not get bigger at the end. It gets quieter and more certain.

### Subtitles

Identical to the voiceover, with one note. The list "Retail. Airlines. Hotels." appears as three short subtitles, each replacing the previous, matching the spoken beat.

### Notes for the storyboard

The visual scale of this section is the inverse of Section ②. We opened the film on intimacy — one purchase, one person. We close it on breadth — twenty-six platforms, every category. The two sections should feel like bookends.

The two sentences of the close are separated by one and a half seconds of silence. That silence is not a pause — it is part of the line. Do not shorten it during the render. The space is the punctuation.

### Subtitles

The tech card on the left does not require subtitles — the names are readable on screen.

## Production notes

### Voice casting

Audition `Rachel` and `Bella` on ElevenLabs first. Both have a conversational register that fits the script. If neither lands, audition `Charlie` and `Sarah` as alternatives. The voice must be capable of softness without becoming sweet, and certainty without becoming dramatic.

If ElevenLabs licensing becomes a concern, audition Kokoro voices via HyperFrames' `hyperframes-media` skill. The fallback should not change the script.

### Render order

The film is rendered as eight separate compositions and stitched at the end. Each composition's source is in `compositions/<section-id>.html`. The stitch order follows Section ① through ⑧ as listed in the index.

### Subtitle styling

Subtitles use Inter Display, weight 500. The base color is white at 95% opacity on most sections; in Section ④ the source-citation line drops to 60% opacity to deprioritize it visually.

### Music and sound

Music is one piece, written or licensed, that holds through the entire film with three changes in dynamic: quiet open (① and ②), restrained middle (③ through ⑦), warm close (⑧). Sound design is layered on top: faint motion at the open, soft chimes for state changes, a single keystroke for the *Approve & Send* moment in 6.7.
